This chapter closes the thesis by revisiting the central objective: to assess whether Large
Language Models (\glspl{llm}) augmented with Retrieval-Augmented Generation (\gls{rag}) can improve
static malware analysis in terms of accuracy, interpretability, and practical usefulness. Motivated
by the limitations of traditional static approaches and the emerging potential of context-grounded
\glspl{llm} (Ch.~\ref{Chapter2}), a dual-pipeline prototype was designed and implemented
(Ch.~\ref{Chapter3}) and evaluated it on a representative cohort (Ch.~\ref{Chapter4}). This chapter
summarises the research, answers the guiding questions, highlights contributions and implications,
discusses limitations, outlines future work, and offers final reflections.

\section{Summary of Research}
% Concisely summarize your overall research journey:
%   - Literature gaps you identified
%   - Your methodological approach
%   - Key components of your system (RAG pipeline, prompt engineering, static feature analysis)
% Keep this section high-level and focused on key ideas, not details.
The literature review (Ch.~\ref{Chapter2}) identified three persistent gaps: limited semantic
understanding in static workflows, weak interpretability in ML-based detectors, and a lack of
evaluations that combine \gls{rag} with \glspl{llm} for malware tasks. To address these, an
experimental, dual-pipeline system (Ch.~\ref{Chapter3}) is implemented: a deterministic baseline
that fuses \gls{ghidra}-derived heuristics with optional \gls{virustotal} tags, and an \gls{llm}
+\gls{rag} pipeline that grounds model reasoning on a curated corpus (VX-Underground APT writeups,
Malpedia documentation, and MalwareBazaar metadata). The \gls{rag} pipeline relied on structured
prompt engineering, guardrails (redaction, evidence thresholding, abstention), and traceable
logging.

Implementation and evaluation (Ch.~\ref{Chapter4}) used 20 Windows \gls{pe} samples across four
categories. Results showed that \textbf{LLM+RAG} improved category accuracy from 10.0\% (2/20) to
27.8\% (5/18 valid classifications; two abstentions), increased explanation quality with 100\%
evidence grounding (\(\geq 2\) citations), and incurred a modest latency increase (90.8\,s to
97.1\,s). A composite score (accuracy, efficiency, interpretability) favoured \textbf{LLM+RAG}
(0.43 vs 0.28), indicating that grounding \glspl{llm} with trustworthy context can tangibly
strengthen static malware analysis.

\textbf{Q1: How can \glspl{llm} be leveraged to enhance static malware analysis?}
\newline
\glspl{llm} complement static feature extraction by synthesising disparate signals (imports,
strings, entropy, \gls{decompilation} snippets) into coherent, analyst-style rationales. In our
prototype, structured prompts and a fixed output schema enabled consistent, auditable
classifications with complete explanation coverage (100\%). Even without fine-tuning, the
\gls{llm} produced actionable summaries and comparisons across malware behaviours, improving
triage utility.

\textbf{Q2: How does a \gls{rag}-enhanced \gls{llm} perform in terms of accuracy, efficiency, and interpretability when compared to traditional static analysis techniques?}
\newline
Against a deterministic baseline, \textbf{LLM+RAG} yielded higher category accuracy (27.8\% vs
10.0\% when skipping abstentions), far stronger interpretability (100\% grounded explanations),
and only a modest latency overhead (\(+\)6.4\,s on average). Guardrails enabled abstention in
low-evidence cases (2/20), avoiding overconfident errors. While absolute accuracy remains modest
due to dataset scope and static-only signals, the relative gains suggest practical benefit in
analyst workflows. A composite score combining accuracy, efficiency, and interpretability also
favoured \textbf{LLM+RAG} (0.43 vs 0.28).

\textbf{Q3: What role does \gls{rag} play in improving \gls{llm}-driven malware classification, particularly in terms of contextual relevance and justification of results?}
\newline
\gls{rag} provided verifiable context that anchored explanations in retrieved evidence (\(\geq 2\)
citations in all cases). Name redaction and re-ranking by technical signals mitigate anchoring
bias. Empirically, \gls{rag} was decisive for explanation quality and for some correct
classifications, supporting the thesis that retrieval-grounded prompts enhance contextual
relevance, justification, and transparency.

\section{Research Contributions}

% Examples:

This thesis contributes a reproducible, dual-pipeline framework for static malware analysis that
systematically contrasts a deterministic baseline against a retrieval-grounded \gls{llm} approach.
The baseline fuses \gls{ghidra}-derived heuristics with optional \gls{virustotal} reconciliation,
providing a transparent reference for comparison. The \gls{llm}+\gls{rag} pipeline integrates
\gls{haystack} over \gls{postgresql}+\gls{pgvector} with \gls{gpt4o} in inference-only mode and a
curated corpus drawn from VX-Underground APT writeups, Malpedia documentation, and MalwareBazaar
metadata. Both pipelines share a unified output schema, structured logging, and prompt versioning
(hashing), exposed via a \texttt{Typer}-based CLI for end-to-end repeatability. An ingestion
pipeline contributes a defensible recipe for building a safe retrieval store for malware analysis:
documents are normalised, chunked, embedded (OpenAI \texttt{text-embedding-3-small}), and stored
with provenance metadata and time windows, enabling auditable retrieval.

The work introduces a guardrailed prompting design tailored to static triage: name redaction to
mitigate anchoring, re-ranking by technical signals, a two-stage ``blind hypothesis then evidence
verification'' flow, and an evidence threshold with principled abstention. Together with a simple
but consistent evaluation protocol and composite metric (accuracy, efficiency, interpretability),
these choices improved explanation reliability and measurement clarity. Empirically, on a fixed
20-sample \gls{pe} cohort, \textbf{LLM+RAG} increased category accuracy from \textbf{10.0\%} (2/20)
to \textbf{27.8\%} (5/18 valid classifications; 2 abstentions), maintained \textbf{100\%}
explanation coverage with \textbf{100\%} contextual grounding (\(\geq\)2 citations), and incurred
only a modest latency increase (\textbf{90.8\,s} to \textbf{97.1\,s}). The composite score improved
from \textbf{0.28} to \textbf{0.43}. Collectively, these contributions provide an auditable
blueprint and quantitative evidence that retrieval-grounded language models can add measurable
value to static malware analysis in research and practitioner settings.

\section{Implications of the Study}\label{sec:Implications}

By demonstrating how \gls{llm} reasoning, when combined with \gls{rag}, enhances the reliability
and transparency of static malware analysis, this work provides evidence that language models can
be adapted to domains traditionally dominated by heuristic rules and signature-based approaches. As
shown in Section~\ref{subsec:ResultsSummary} (Table~\ref{tab:results-summary}), the
\gls{llm}+\gls{rag} pipeline improved accuracy and explanation quality with a modest latency
overhead.

\subsection{Short-term implications}\label{subsec:ShortTermImplications}
In the short term, the results suggest that integrating retrieval-enhanced language models into
analysis pipelines can improve the quality of malware classification without prohibitive efficiency
trade-offs (see Section~\ref{sec:Limitations}). The consistent grounding of explanations in
external evidence enables analysts to validate model outputs and trace the rationale behind
predictions. This supports the development of explainable malware analysis tools, where
transparency is as critical as accuracy. Practically, LLM+RAG can operate as a triage gate:
high-confidence outputs (with citations) are auto-labelled, while abstentions (\texttt{unknown}) or
low-confidence cases are routed to analysts. Organisations can target measurable improvements, such
as a \textit{15--25\% reduction}\footnote{This projection is an extrapolation from the 20-sample
    evaluation suite used in this study. It assumes that the observed rates of explanation coverage
    (100\%) and abstention behaviour (2/20 samples) would scale proportionally in larger datasets.
    While indicative of potential operational gains, these figures should be interpreted as
    illustrative rather than empirically validated benchmarks.} in manual reviews per day or a
\textit{10--15\% reduction} in median time-to-triage, driven by 100\% explanation coverage and
grounding in our results (cf.~Table~\ref{tab:results-summary}). In operational contexts, such tools
can reduce analyst fatigue by filtering and prioritising evidence-backed alerts, complementing
traditional static features such as \gls{api} calls and binary structure.

\subsection{Operational support}\label{subsec:OperationalSupport}
For security teams, the potential of explainable classification is particularly important. Unlike
rule-based systems that may generate opaque alerts, \gls{llm}+\gls{rag} outputs provide contextual
citations and clear rationales for decisions. This improves trust and accelerates incident triage
by linking observed behaviour to cited intelligence. To integrate with SOC tooling, pipelines
should export structured fields (category, rationale, citations, confidence/abstention) to
SIEM/SOAR/MISP in JSON or STIX~2.1. Calibration is essential: apply thresholding, honour
abstentions (no auto-block on \textit{suspicious} without corroboration), and log decisions for
auditability. Privacy and compliance should be maintained by restricting the \gls{rag} corpus to
metadata/prose (no binaries) and filtering sensitive data via guardrails
(cf.~Section~\ref{sec:Guardrails}). Over time, such capabilities can help close the expertise gap
in malware analysis by lowering cognitive overhead for less experienced practitioners.

\subsection{Cross-domain applications}\label{subsec:CrossDomainApplications}
Beyond malware classification, the principles demonstrated here extend to cybersecurity tasks where
static artefacts are abundant but context is fragmented. Examples include phishing analysis that
explains why a lure is suspicious, or CVE advisory triage that prioritises advisories based on
retrieved PoCs and exploitation reports, with citations for audit. Prior work has highlighted the
role of retrieval in broadening the contextual window for \glspl{llm}~\cite{gao2023retrieval,
    lewis2020retrieval}, and this study confirms its value in static-first analysis. Future research
could adapt similar architectures to threat intelligence enrichment or automated reporting.

\subsection{Long-term implications}\label{subsec:LongTermImplications}
In the longer term, the study suggests a path towards hybrid workflows where \glspl{llm} act as
interpreters and equalisers of context rather than decision-makers. This aligns with emerging
trends in explainable AI for cybersecurity~\cite{fujii2024llm, nourmohammadzadeh2024llmsurvey}. By
grounding outputs in verifiable references, the risk of unchecked hallucinations is reduced, while
the system remains adaptable to evolving threats. Operationally, organisations should plan for
corpus curation (periodic re-ingestion and de-duplication), prompt/guardrail versioning, and
continuous evaluation (re-running the fixed 20-sample suite plus a growing benchmark quarterly) to
monitor accuracy, abstention, and latency trends. Governance should document decision policies
(when to abstain/escalate), change management for prompts/guardrails, and audit trails (leveraging
the existing metadata logging and prompt hashes in this project). From an academic perspective,
these findings strengthen the case for bridging static feature extraction and semantic reasoning,
paving the way for more resilient and interpretable detection pipelines.

While the study demonstrates the potential of \gls{llm}+\gls{rag} in static malware analysis, key
limitations remain. The cohort was small (\textbf{n=20}; five per tag) and limited to Windows
\gls{pe} executables (\texttt{exe}), yielding wide Wilson 95\% CIs
(Section~\ref{subsec:ResultsSummary}) and limited generalisability. The scope was static-only (no
behavioural/dynamic features). The \textbf{Baseline} used brittle heuristics with under-calibrated
thresholds and likely underestimates stronger traditional baselines.

Ground truth followed cohort selection via a helper script against the \gls{malwarebazaar} \gls{api},
recording acquisition tags as a proxy for consensus~\cite{rexisSamplesExtractorScript}. This
carries vendor noise and ambiguity in canonical category mapping~\cite{gaber2024malware}. Accuracy
was computed \textit{excluding} abstentions; as a sensitivity check, counting abstentions as errors
would reduce \textbf{LLM+RAG} to \textbf{25.0\%} (5/20) vs \textbf{Baseline} \textbf{10.0\%}
(2/20). Composite scoring used equal weights; different weightings could change the ranking.

Retrieval quality depended on a corpus from VX-Underground, Malpedia, and MalwareBazaar for
Jan\,2022--Aug\,2025 (Section~\ref{subsec:RAGIngestionExec}), which may miss relevant threats;
private or region-specific sources were not included, and drift/re-ingestion effects were not
studied. Prompts and guardrails were intentionally simple (Section~\ref{sec:PromptEngineering}); no
hyperparameter or guardrail sensitivity was run. Provider-side variability and nondeterminism
remain despite fixed pipeline settings (Section~\ref{sec:ExperimentalSteps}).

Operationally, the system depends on third-party APIs (\gls{malwarebazaar}, \gls{vt}, \gls{openai})
with implications for cost, rate limits, and data handling. Token usage for \gls{gpt4o} was not
analysed. Privacy was preserved by indexing metadata/prose only; guardrails were not formally
tested against adversarial threats (e.g., prompt injection).

These limitations should be addressed in future work to enhance the robustness and applicability of
\gls{llm}+\gls{rag} for malware analysis.

This study demonstrates the feasibility of \gls{llm}+\gls{rag} for static malware analysis and
points to clear next steps: broaden datasets, enrich retrieval sources, refine models/prompts, and
validate in operational settings.

\subsection{Hybrid Pipelines}\label{sec:HybridPipelines}
Future work should extend beyond static analysis and explore hybrid pipelines that combine static
features with behavioural or dynamic traces. Prior work has shown that hybrid approaches can
improve detection resilience against packing and obfuscation~\cite{fedak2020fundamentals}, and
\glspl{llm} are well positioned to synthesise heterogeneous evidence into coherent explanations.
This would allow evaluation not only of \gls{pe} executables but also scripts, documents, and
platform-specific binaries (e.g., Android APKs, macOS Mach-O).

\subsection{Larger and Labelled Datasets}\label{sec:LargerAndLabelledDatasets}
Scaling to larger, labelled datasets is essential for statistical robustness. Public resources such
as EMBER~\cite{anderson2018ember} or curated subsets of \gls{malwarebazaar} could provide stronger
ground truth for benchmarking. Community-driven datasets with canonical category mappings would
also help mitigate vendor noise~\cite{gaber2024malware}. In parallel, systematic evaluations of
abstentions should be incorporated into accuracy metrics to ensure fair comparisons across models.

\subsection{Model and Prompt Design}\label{sec:ModelAndPromptDesign}
Alternative \glspl{llm} should be considered, including open-source models fine-tuned for
cybersecurity tasks~\cite{metta2024generativeai, nourmohammadzadeh2024llmsurvey}. Fine-tuning on
domain-specific corpora, or applying structured prompting techniques~\cite{sahoo2024systematic},
may further improve classification accuracy and reduce abstentions. Sensitivity analyses of
prompting, guardrails, and hyperparameters would provide insights into the stability of results
over time.

\subsection{Improving Retrieval Quality}\label{sec:ImprovingRetrievalQuality}
Retrieval quality is a key factor in performance. Future research should investigate improved
chunking strategies, embedding models optimised for binary-derived text, and active curation of the
retrieval corpus to reduce drift. Richer sources (e.g., telemetry feeds, red-team reports) could
increase coverage and contextual relevance~\cite{gao2023retrieval, lewis2020retrieval}.

\subsection{Real-world Evaluation}\label{sec:RealWorldEvaluation}
Finally, deployment-oriented studies are needed to assess the utility of \gls{llm}+\gls{rag} in
operational environments such as SOCs. Integration with SIEM/SOAR/MISP platforms, combined with
analyst-in-the-loop evaluations, would help measure usability, trust, and incident-response impact.
Red-team exercises could probe adversarial robustness, including resistance to prompt injection and
retrieval poisoning~\cite{zou2024poisonedrag}. Such evaluations would move beyond feasibility to
assess readiness for production deployment.

\section{Final Reflections}

This thesis shows that retrieval-grounded \glspl{llm} can add measurable value to static malware
analysis—framed by the literature and problem statement (Ch.~\ref{Chapter2}), realised via a
dual-pipeline prototype with guardrails (Ch.~\ref{Chapter3}), and validated through a reproducible
evaluation (Ch.~\ref{Chapter4}). It is not a replacement for established tools; rather, it is a
context synthesiser that elevates interpretability and supports trustworthy decisions. The
prototype, guardrails, and evaluation framework provide a pragmatic blueprint for researchers and
practitioners to iterate, harden, and scale similar systems. While challenges remain—scope,
robustness, and cost—the path forward is clear: combine reliable feature extraction with grounded
language understanding, and hold models accountable to evidence. In doing so, the community moves
closer to explainable, auditable, and effective AI support for cybersecurity operations.
