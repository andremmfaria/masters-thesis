\chapter{Methodology}\label{sec:Method}\label{Chapter3} % For referencing the chapter elsewhere, use \ref{Chapter3}

% architecture, evaluation strategy, and toolchain.
This chapter outlines the methodology used to implement and evaluate a prototype system for static
\gls{malware} analysis enhanced with \gls{llm}-based reasoning and \gls{rag} integration. The study
follows a dual-pipeline approach designed to assess and compare two independent methods of
\gls{malware} classification: a baseline static tagging classifier, and an LLM-driven \gls{rag}
pipeline. Both pipelines ingest the same malware samples and execute in parallel, enabling a
structured comparison of classification quality, context relevance, and explanation richness. No
model \emph{fine-tuning} is applied; instead, emphasis is placed on structured prompt engineering
and the injection of contextual knowledge through retrieval mechanisms.

The feasibility of using \glspl{llm} to support static feature-based malware analysis is grounded
in recent work by Fujii and Yamagishi~\cite{fujii2024llm}, who demonstrated that LLMs such as
\gls{gpt4o} are capable of interpreting decompiled code and extracted metadata to support
classification tasks. Their study serves as an empirical justification for using generative models
as reasoning agents in static malware pipelines.

Further support comes from Nourmohammadzadeh et al.~\cite{nourmohammadzadeh2024llmsurvey}, who
provide a state-of-the-art review of \gls{llm} applications in cybersecurity, demonstrating their
increasing role in vulnerability detection, anomaly analysis, and interpretability-focused tools.
Ferrag et al.~\cite{ferrag2024allinsights} and Metta et al.~\cite{metta2024generativeai} complement
this perspective by identifying the strategic integration of \glspl{llm} and \glspl{rag} as a
transformative step for cyberdefense pipelines.

To benchmark the LLM-enhanced pipeline, this study implements a separate rule-based classifier that
draws from \gls{virustotal} tagging and local heuristics using tools like \gls{peframe}. This
classifier represents a traditional static analysis method and allows for comparative assessment
without retraining machine learning models. This design is inspired by practitioner workflows
documented by Wong et al.~\cite{wong2021practice}, who emphasize the role of interpretable
heuristics in malware triage workflows.

The risks introduced by \gls{rag} systems, including the possibility of knowledge poisoning and
answer manipulation, are addressed with insights from Zou et al.~\cite{zou2024poisonedrag}, who
present the PoisonedRAG attack framework. Their findings are used to justify the inclusion of a
compliance module to ensure grounding integrity during evaluation.

\section{Research Design}\label{sec:RresearchDesign}
% Describe the nature of your research: exploratory, experimental, comparative.
% Explain the reasoning for using a prototype-based implementation and evaluation.
% Emphasize that your approach avoids LLM fine-tuning and instead relies on
% prompt engineering and external context via RAG.
The nature of this research is primarily experimental and exploratory, motivated by the need to
evaluate whether \glspl{llm}, when paired with \gls{retrieval-augmented-generation} (\gls{rag}),
can enhance static \gls{malware} analysis. Rather than relying on pre-trained classifiers or
supervised models trained on static datasets, this study adopts a \textbf{prototype-based
    dual-pipeline design} to enable practical evaluation across two independent approaches:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Static Tag-Based Classification (Baseline)}: A lightweight
          rule-based and heuristic classifier is developed, incorporating tag
          inference from \gls{virustotal} and static analysis tools such as
          \gls{peframe}. This baseline emulates the kind of signature and
          metadata-based triage used by malware analysts in practice\cite{wong2021practice}.
    \item \textbf{LLM + RAG-Assisted Classification}: This pipeline uses a
          \gls{haystacks}-based \gls{rag} framework to retrieve relevant context
          documents from a \gls{pgvector}-enabled \gls{postgresql} database.
          These documents, alongside extracted malware features, are formatted into
          structured prompts and passed to an \gls{llm} (e.g., \gls{gpt4o} or
          \gls{deepseek-r1}) for classification, explanation, and contextual analysis.
\end{itemize}

This design leverages the findings from Fujii and Yamagishi~\cite{fujii2024llm}, who confirmed the
potential of general-purpose \glspl{llm} to interpret static malware features. It also aligns with
Wong et al.~\cite{wong2021practice}, who detail how practitioners depend on quick static
classifiers and heuristics for initial triage.

Rather than conducting fine-tuning or supervised retraining, both pipelines are evaluated
\textbf{in inference-only mode}. This decision is supported by Gao et al.~\cite{gao2023retrieval},
who emphasize that \gls{rag} can meaningfully improve reasoning performance in \glspl{llm} without
retraining by injecting high-quality external context into prompts.

To address the risks of knowledge hallucination or poisoning, highlighted by Zou et
al.\cite{zou2024poisonedrag}, a compliance module is integrated before inference to filter
sensitive or misleading content from retrieved documents. The entire process is logged in both
pipelines to ensure traceability and enable performance comparison.

\subsection{Experimental Workflow}\label{sec:ExperimentalWorkflow}

Each malware sample is independently passed through both pipelines in parallel. The goal is to
generate comparable outputs under consistent input conditions, simplifying parallel benchmarking,
and providing a basis for evaluating the contributions of \gls{llm}-based reasoning to traditional
static analysis pipelines.

The following workflow is applied to each sample:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Static Feature Extraction:} Common to both pipelines, static
          features are extracted using \texttt{pefile}, \gls{peframe}, and custom
          Python scripts. These include:
          \begin{itemize}
              \item PE headers and section metadata
              \item Imported \gls{api} calls and DLLs
              \item Section entropy, resource entries, and printable strings
          \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Pipeline 1 --~Static Classifier (Baseline):}
          \begin{itemize}
              \item Extracted features are matched against heuristic rules (e.g., suspicious imports, entropy
                    thresholds).
              \item If a \gls{virustotal} tag is available via API, it is mapped to a standardized malware family
                    label.
              \item Outputs include classification label, triggered heuristics, and tagging confidence.
          \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Pipeline 2 --~LLM + RAG Analysis:}
          \begin{itemize}
              \item Static features are used to query a pipeline built with Python with the \gls{haystacks} framework
                    and \gls{postgresql} as datastore with the \gls{pgvector} plugin. It will serve to retrieve
                    relevant documents (e.g., malware family reports, documentation).
              \item Retrieved context and extracted features are embedded into a prompt template.
              \item Prompt is passed to an \gls{llm} (e.g., \gls{gpt4o} or \gls{deepseek-r1}) via API.\
              \item Output includes classification label, explanation, and reasoning trace.
          \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Logging and Output Storage:}
          \begin{itemize}
              \item For both pipelines, all outputs (classification, reasoning/tags, latency, model info) are logged
                    using a structured schema. Each entry includes:
                    \begin{itemize}
                        \item Sample hash (e.g., SHA256)
                        \item Pipeline name (Baseline or LLM+RAG)
                        \item Classification result
                        \item Confidence score (if available)
                        \item Explanation or triggered rule (s)
                        \item Timestamp and runtime metadata
                    \end{itemize}
              \item Each sample's results are indexed by hash and timestamp to allow reproducibility and evaluation.
          \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Malware Sample Ingestion}\label{subsec:MalwareSampleIngestion}
The system accepts samples in \gls{pe} format, including '.exe', '.dll', and '.sys' binaries, which
are commonly used by malware on the Windows platform. The decision to focus on \gls{pe} files is
grounded in both practical and research-driven considerations.\ \gls{pe} files represent the
dominant binary format for Windows-based malware, which remains the most targeted operating system
by malicious actors worldwide. Their structure is well-documented and rich in analyzable metadata,
including headers, sections, and import tables. Another reason is that numerous tools such as
\texttt{pefile}, \texttt{peframe}, and \gls{ghidra} provide strong support for static feature
extraction from PE files. As noted by Fujii and Yamagishi~\cite{fujii2024llm}, static analysis of
PE files yields sufficient signal for classification tasks when combined with external context,
particularly for exploratory research involving \glspl{llm}.

Samples are sourced from public repositories such as \gls{malwarebazaar}, or retrieved via API from
\gls{virustotal}. Each binary is assigned a unique identifier (SHA256 hash) and stored in a local
database for processing.

\subsection{Static Feature Extraction with Ghidra}\label{subsec:StaticFeatureExtraction}
For both pipelines, the first step involves extracting static features from each sample. This is
performed using an automated Ghidra server instance controlled via \texttt{pyghidra}, which enables
scripting of batch decompilation and analysis. Features extracted include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item PE header metadata (e.g., compile timestamp, entry point, number of sections)
    \item Imported \gls{api} functions and linked libraries
    \item Section structure and entropy
    \item Embedded printable strings
    \item Decompiled function names and control flow structure (where available)
\end{itemize}
All extracted features are logged to a local structured data store and used by
both pipelines. The use of Ghidra ensures consistency across samples and
provides a deep view into malware internals, even in the absence of obfuscation~\cite{fujii2024llm}.

\subsection{Pipeline A --~Static Tag-Based Classification (Baseline)}\label{subsec:StaticTagBasedClassification}
The baseline pipeline uses static features and VirusTotal tags to classify malware samples,
following standard analyst workflows. It combines heuristic rule matching (e.g., suspicious API
imports, entropy checks) with tag mapping from VirusTotal to assign malware family labels. For a
detailed description of this process, see Section~\ref{subsec:BaselinePipeline}.

\subsection{Pipeline B --~LLM + RAG-Based Classification}\label{subsec:RAGBasedClassification}
This pipeline enhances static analysis by integrating \gls{rag} and \gls{llm}. It uses a vector
database (\gls{postgresql} + \gls{pgvector}) to store embedded threat intelligence documents, and
the Haystack framework to retrieve relevant context based on extracted static features. Retrieved
documents and features are combined into a structured prompt, filtered for compliance, and
submitted to an LLM (e.g., \gls{gpt4o} or \gls{deepseek-r1}) for classification and explanation.
For full technical details, see Section~\ref{subsec:LLMRAGPipeline}.

\subsection{Logging and Result Storage}\label{subsec:LoggingAndResult}
Both pipelines store their outputs in a structured format with fields for:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sample ID (SHA256), timestamp, and pipeline type
    \item Classification result, explanation, and associated metadata
    \item Latency measurements and source references
\end{itemize}
These results are later used in the evaluation framework (Section~\ref{sec:EvaluationFramework}).

\section{Data Sources}\label{sec:DataSources}

As shown in the System Architecture diagram (Figure~\ref{fig:system_architecture}), the analysis
begins with \gls{malware} samples and diverges into two paths—one leveraging rule-based static
classification, and the other combining feature-based semantic querying with \gls{llm} inference.

\subsection{Malware Samples Sourcing}\label{subsec:MalwareSamplesSourcing}
The primary input to the system is a corpus of \gls{pe} files. This file format, standard for
Windows executables (e.g., \texttt{.exe}, \texttt{.dll}), is widely used in static \gls{malware}
analysis due to its rich structural features such as headers, import/export tables, and embedded
strings\cite{fedak2020fundamentals}.

Feature extraction will be performed using \gls{ghidra}, a reverse engineering tool capable of
decompilation and structural analysis. Automation is handled through
PyGhidra\footnote{\url{https://github.com/NationalSecurityAgency/ghidra/blob/master/Ghidra/Features/PyGhidra/src/main/py/README.md}},
enabling programmatic access to low-level features. This ensures the uniform extraction of relevant
artifacts for both pipelines.

Malware samples are sourced from:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{\gls{malwarebazaar}}: An open repository maintained by
          abuse.ch that provides recent PE malware samples for research purposes.
    \item \textbf{\gls{virustotal}}: A commercial platform offering API-based
          access to \gls{malware} samples, including scan metadata and tags.
\end{itemize}

These PE files are stored and catalogued with timestamp and hash-based identifiers.

\subsection{Baseline Metadata Sources}\label{subsec:BaselineSources}
For the baseline classification pipeline, a hybrid strategy combining heuristics and third-party
metadata is employed. The idea is to provide a deterministic classification model that does not
rely on \gls{llm} capabilities, offering a contrast point for evaluation.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rule-Based Heuristics}: Static rules are applied using tools
          such as \gls{peframe} and \texttt{pefile}. These extract and match against
          known patterns (e.g., suspicious section names, use of packers, API
          imports).
    \item \textbf{VirusTotal Tag Mapping}: Labels and family tags from
          VirusTotal scans are queried via the public API and mapped to categories
          such as ransomware, trojan, downloader, or dropper, following established
          taxonomies~\cite{gaber2024malware}.
\end{itemize}

The output from these sources feeds into a custom baseline classifier module (see
Figure~\ref{fig:system_architecture}, "Baseline Classifier"), used to generate human-readable
justifications and assign threat categories without any inference-based reasoning.

\subsection{RAG Corpus Construction}\label{subsec:RAGCorpusConstruction}
To support the \gls{rag} pipeline, a dedicated corpus is constructed to supply contextual documents
during inference. This corpus is not retrieved from a pre-existing vector database but instead
populated manually using relevant, high-quality sources. The goal is to include rich semantic
context that can support static malware classification and interpretation. This corpus is creation
is done via a data ingestion pipeline, described on section~~\ref{subsec:RAGIngestionPipeline}.

\vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Corpus Sources}: The following publicly available sources are used to gather data for embedding:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{VX-Underground APT Writeups}: A curated collection of Advanced
          Persistent Threat (APT) reports, reverse engineering notes, and malware
          technical breakdowns\footnote{\url{https://vx-underground.org/APTs}}.
          These documents provide real-world adversary tactics, file structures,
          and static artifacts used by sophisticated actors.

    \item \textbf{Malpedia Family Documentation}: Structured descriptions of
          known malware families, including behavioral traits, IOCs, and YARA
          rules\footnote{\url{https://malpedia.caad.fkie.fraunhofer.de/}}.
          These documents are particularly useful for understanding static code
          traits across known malware variants.

    \item \textbf{MalwareBazaar Sample Metadata}: Sample submissions from
          analysts and researchers, containing static features such as file hashes,
          file types, tags, and AV detection results\footnote{\url{https://bazaar.abuse.ch/}}.
          While raw binaries are not directly included in the RAG context, this
          metadata serves as classification-relevant context for LLM consumption.

    \item \textbf{VirusTotal API Metadata}: Using the public or academic
          VirusTotal API, sample metadata including AV engine tags, signature matches,
          and file type characteristics is
          retrieved\footnote{\url{https://www.virustotal.com/gui/home/search}}.
          These structured elements are formatted into documents suitable for embedding.
\end{itemize}

Documents are cleaned, segmented, and embedded using the Haystack framework. Each document includes
metadata fields such as source URL, family name, type (APT writeup, YARA rule, sample metadata),
and timestamp to enable filtering and traceability. The processed embeddings are stored in the
\gls{pgvector}-enabled \gls{postgresql} datastore (see Figure~\ref{fig:rag_ingestion}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{images/data-ingestion.png}
    \caption{RAG Corpus Construction Pipeline}\label{fig:rag_ingestion}
\end{figure}

The preprocessing step handles document normalization, metadata cleaning, and deduplication.
Documents are then chunked into manageable units, encoded via language model embeddings (e.g.,
OpenAI's embedding API), and finally stored in the vector database (\texttt{PostgreSQL + pgvector})
using Haystack.

This design enables retrieval of semantically similar content during inference-time, which is then
combined into structured prompts for the \gls{llm}, following best practices outlined in recent
surveys on \gls{rag} systems~\cite{gao2023retrieval, lewis2020retrieval}.

\subsection{Script-Based Malware (LLM-Only Sample)}\label{subsec:ScriptMalware}

To demonstrate the adaptability of the \gls{llm}-based pipeline, a script-based malware sample is
introduced. Unlike PE files, this category includes threats written in high-level scripting
languages (e.g., PowerShell, VBScript, or JavaScript) which are unsuitable for static PE-based
heuristics.

The sample will be manually selected and analyzed exclusively using the RAG+LLM pipeline. This
allows for:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Demonstration of \gls{llm} capability beyond structured binary analysis
    \item Evaluation of reasoning and explanation features on loosely structured inputs
\end{itemize}

Such flexibility is critical given the increasing use of living-off-the-land (LotL) and fileless
malware techniques, as noted in recent cybersecurity reviews \cite{ferrag2024allinsights,
    metta2024generativeai}.

\subsection{Ground Truth and Labeling}\label{subsec:Labeling}
For evaluation purposes, all samples are annotated using VirusTotal metadata and matched against
open-source labeling conventions where available. While this does not represent a perfect ground
truth, it provides a consistent benchmark to compare classification accuracy and explainability
across both pipelines.

\subsection{Baseline Pipeline}\label{subsec:BaselinePipeline}
% Covers the non-LLM static pipeline:
% - Input format (PE files).
% - Feature extraction via Ghidra (e.g., strings, imported functions).
% - Logging and preprocessing.
% - Hybrid classifier:
%   - **Heuristics**: Signature-based and rule-based logic.
%   - **VirusTotal**: Tag correlation and family mapping.
% - Output classification format.

The baseline pipeline represents a traditional static malware classification process that does not
rely on \glspl{llm}. It is designed to provide a deterministic, explainable benchmark against which
the \gls{rag}-enhanced pipeline can be compared. This pipeline processes \gls{pe} files and
produces a static classification label using a hybrid method combining custom heuristics with
VirusTotal's family tags.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Input Format:} The pipeline operates exclusively on \glspl{pe},
the standard format for executables in Windows environments. These files were
selected due to their structural regularity and prevalence in real-world malware
datasets, which allows for consistent feature extraction and rule-based
analysis~\cite{fedak2020fundamentals}.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Feature Extraction:} Using \gls{ghidra} and its Python
automation layer \texttt{PyGhidra}, key static features are extracted from each
binary~\cite{fedak2020fundamentals, fujii2024llm}. These features include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Extracted \texttt{.text}, \texttt{.data}, and \texttt{.rsrc} sections
    \item Strings and entropy calculations
    \item Imported function calls and dynamic-link library (DLL) names
    \item Entry point location and PE header metadata
\end{itemize}

These features are logged into structured \texttt{pandas} dataframes for downstream processing.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Preprocessing and Logging:} Extracted values are normalized,
deduplicated, and serialized into tabular form for processing. The logs are kept
both for auditability and reproducibility, and serve as inputs to the
classification stage.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Hybrid Classification Strategy:} The core classification step
is composed of two components:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rule-Based Heuristics:} A set of hand-crafted rules assess static indicators, such as:
          \begin{itemize}
              \item Specific API imports (e.g., \texttt{CreateRemoteThread}, \texttt{VirtualAllocEx})
              \item High entropy in packed sections
              \item Presence of suspicious strings or obfuscation markers
          \end{itemize}
          Based on these features, heuristic labels (e.g., ``ransomware-like'',
          ``downloader'') are assigned.~\cite{gaber2024malware,
              cassanova2022systematic}.

    \item \textbf{VirusTotal Tag Mapping:} For each file, the \gls{virustotal}
          API is queried using its SHA-256 hash. Family tags (e.g., \texttt{Emotet},
          \texttt{AgentTesla}) and antivirus engine classifications are retrieved. A
          mapping layer harmonizes divergent vendor names into canonical families,
          based on predefined normalization rules.~\cite{wong2021practice}.
\end{itemize}

The final label is determined by reconciling both sources: if heuristic and \gls{virustotal} tags
align, the label is accepted as-is; if they differ, precedence is given to the VirusTotal tag
unless the heuristic confidence exceeds a defined threshold.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Output Format:} The resulting classification includes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Canonical family label (e.g., \texttt{LokiBot})
    \item Confidence score (from heuristic analysis)
    \item Relevant rules triggered
    \item VirusTotal engine match count
\end{itemize}

This format is structured to facilitate side-by-side comparison with the \gls{llm} pipeline output.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Technologies Used:} The baseline pipeline is implemented using:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{PyGhidra} --~Automates static feature extraction within \gls{ghidra}
    \item \textbf{Pandas} --~For structured tabular logging and feature processing
    \item \textbf{Heuristic Engine} --~Rule-based decision logic written for this experiment
    \item \textbf{VirusTotal API} --~External enrichment with malware family tags and detections
\end{itemize}

\subsection{LLM+RAG Pipeline}\label{subsec:LLMRAGPipeline}

The LLM+RAG pipeline integrates large language models with retrieval-augmented generation to
enhance static malware classification. Unlike the baseline pipeline, it leverages semantic
retrieval over a curated threat corpus to provide context-aware inference, enabling explainable and
more flexible analysis. This method focuses on inference-time reasoning rather than learned
classification logic, facilitating zero-shot generalization and reducing the need for fine-tuning.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Input Format and Feature Extraction:} The pipeline begins
identically to the baseline.\ \gls{pe} file is processed using \gls{ghidra} and
\texttt{PyGhidra} to extract static features such as:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Imported APIs and DLLs
    \item Decompiled string literals
    \item Entropy and section metadata
\end{itemize}
These features are logged and preprocessed using \texttt{pandas} and serialized
for semantic use in query generation.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Semantic Query Generation:} Using \gls{haystacks}, key
extracted features are concatenated into a structured natural language query.
For example, imported APIs and extracted strings are transformed into a
sentence-level description of behavior (e.g., “This binary uses networking and
injection-related APIs and contains obfuscated payload strings.”). This query
serves as the basis for semantic search.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Contextual Document Retrieval:} A retrieval component searches
a pre-built vector store implemented using \gls{postgresql} and the
\texttt{pgvector} extension. The vector store is populated with curated
malware-related documents such as technical APT reports, annotated
disassemblies, and malware family descriptions. Documents are embedded using
models such as \texttt{text-embedding-3-small}, and indexed via cosine
similarity. The top-$k$ semantically similar documents are retrieved and logged
alongside their similarity scores, latency, and embedding metadata. This
retrieval mechanism is consistent with findings by Gao et
al.~\cite{gao2023retrieval}, which demonstrate the effectiveness of grounding
\glspl{llm} in external structured knowledge.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Prompt Construction and Compliance Filtering:} Retrieved
documents are summarized and embedded into a structured prompt template
alongside the extracted static features. The prompt template instructs the model
to:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Classify the sample into a known malware family
    \item Justify the classification using contextual evidence
    \item Compare with similar malware if applicable
\end{itemize}
Prior to submission, the prompt is filtered through a compliance layer that
removes hex dumps, shellcode fragments, and sensitive indicators. This step
follows best practices to mitigate prompt injection and hallucination as
discussed by Zou et al.~\cite{zou2024poisonedrag}.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{LLM Inference and Response Handling:} The filtered prompt is
submitted to either \gls{gpt4o} via the OpenAI API or \gls{deepseek-r1} via the
DeepSeek API. Models are used strictly in inference mode—no fine-tuning is
applied—emphasizing prompt-based generalization for flexible classification. The
output includes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A proposed malware family label (e.g., \texttt{RedLine}, \texttt{CobaltStrike})
    \item Explanation of how retrieved documents support the classification
    \item Optional comparison with known samples or behaviors
\end{itemize}

Prompt metadata, model identifiers, latency, and token usage are logged for reproducibility and
performance analysis.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Output Format:} The output is formatted in parallel to the baseline, including:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Family label and supporting rationale
    \item Retrieved documents (IDs and titles)
    \item Token usage and model identifier
\end{itemize}
This structure ensures alignment with the baseline for comparative evaluation.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Technologies Used:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ghidra + PyGhidra} --~Static feature extraction
    \item \textbf{Haystack} --~Semantic query orchestration and vector search
    \item \textbf{PostgreSQL + pgvector} --~Retrieval backend and document index
    \item \textbf{OpenAI API / DeepSeek API} --~Inference endpoint for \glspl{llm}
    \item \textbf{Pandas} --~Preprocessing, prompt serialization, and logging
\end{itemize}

\subsection{RAG Ingestion Pipeline}\label{subsec:RAGIngestionPipeline}

To support contextual retrieval in the \gls{rag}-enabled classification pipeline, a dedicated
ingestion workflow is implemented to populate the underlying vector store with structured,
semantically rich documents. This ingestion pipeline is responsible for fetching, cleaning,
embedding, and storing contextual data from multiple threat intelligence sources.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Input Data Sources:} Four primary sources are used to build the
corpus:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{VX-Underground APT Writeups} --~Public APT reports containing
          adversary TTPs, binary structure notes, and malware reverse engineering
          documentation.
    \item \textbf{Malpedia Documentation} --~Structured malware family
          descriptions and behavioral summaries.
    \item \textbf{MalwareBazaar Metadata} --~Tag-based and hash-level metadata
          for malware samples submitted by the research community.
    \item \textbf{VirusTotal Metadata} --~Retrieved via API, including antivirus
          family tags, behavioral signatures, and heuristic detections.
\end{itemize}

These documents are collected using custom Python scripts or RESTful API queries and stored
temporarily in JSON format prior to preprocessing.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Preprocessing and Cleaning:} All raw documents undergo the
following transformations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Language normalization (UTF-8 encoding, newline/whitespace trimming)
    \item Removal of duplicate entries and empty content blocks
    \item Anonymization of private data such as internal IP addresses or analyst handles
    \item Document type and source labeling via metadata fields
\end{itemize}

Structured metadata is appended to each document, including the document source, timestamp, family
label (if known), and threat category (e.g., info-stealer, loader, trojan).

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Chunking Strategy:} Once cleaned, each document is split into
semantically coherent chunks using Haystack's \texttt{TextSplitter}. This
ensures each chunk remains within the token limit of the embedding model and
preserves context. The chunking parameters are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maximum chunk size: 512 tokens
    \item Overlap: 20--50 tokens to preserve semantic continuity
    \item Chunk granularity: sentence-level where possible
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Embedding and Vectorization:} Chunks are encoded using the
embedding model selected for the project. The primary model is:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{OpenAI \texttt{text-embedding-3-small}} --~Embedding model
          used via OpenAI API for generating high-dimensional vectors suited for
          semantic retrieval. Alternatives such as DeepSeek or sentence-transformers
          may be used for ablation studies.
\end{itemize}
Each embedding is paired with its corresponding metadata and stored for vector
search.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Database Insertion:} Embedded chunks and metadata are stored in
\gls{postgresql} using the \texttt{pgvector} extension, which enables fast
cosine similarity search. Haystack's document store integration handles
insertion, index creation, and metadata filtering. Each entry contains:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Vector representation
    \item Original text chunk
    \item Source metadata (e.g., filename, label, source URL)
    \item Embedding version and timestamp
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Tools and Orchestration:} The ingestion pipeline is implemented
in Python using the following components:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Haystack Framework} --~For chunking, embedding, and insertion
    \item \textbf{OpenAI API} --~For embedding generation
    \item \textbf{Custom Scripts} --~For source collection, normalization, and format conversion
    \item \textbf{PDM} --~For environment management and reproducibility
\end{itemize}

\section{Prompt Engineering}\label{sec:PromptEngineering}
% Discuss how you craft prompts for different tasks: classification, explanation, and comparison.
% Include examples of how static features and retrieved context are structured in the prompt.
% Explain the decision to use GPT-4 and/or DeepSeek-R1 in inference-only mode.
The design of effective prompts is central to the performance of the \gls{rag}-enhanced pipeline.
This section outlines the prompt construction methodology used throughout the study, including the
integration of retrieved documents, static features, and classification instructions. The goal is
to transform raw malware data and semantic context into a structured input format that maximizes
interpretability, consistency, and task relevance during inference.

Unlike traditional fine-tuning approaches, this study adopts a zero-shot generalization strategy.
All prompts are crafted manually or via templates and submitted to hosted \glspl{llm} in
inference-only mode. This design promotes reproducibility and avoids training-induced biases,
aligning with the research goal of evaluating LLM reasoning under minimal assumptions.

\subsection{Prompt Structure}\label{subsec:PromptStructure}
Prompts are built using a modular template system, which concatenates static features, retrieved
documents, and task instructions into a unified prompt. The structure follows a three-part format:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Context Section}: Includes extracted static features (e.g.,
          imported API calls, entropy indicators, detected packers) presented as
          natural language bullet points. This is followed by a set of up to $k=3$
          summarized documents retrieved via the \gls{haystacks} semantic search
          module. Each document is labeled with its source and a short
          system-generated summary.

    \item \textbf{Instruction Block}: A fixed instruction prompt requests three
          outputs: (1) malware family classification, (2) justification based on
          features and retrieved context, and (3) a comparison with known malware
          behaviors or tactics.

    \item \textbf{Metadata Footer}: A short metadata footer is appended,
          including model ID placeholder, timestamp, and token count estimate. This
          block is not sent to the model but used internally for audit and
          reproducibility purposes.
\end{enumerate}

An example prompt excerpt is shown below:

\begin{quote}
    \texttt{Context:}
    - The file imports \texttt{CreateRemoteThread}, \texttt{VirtualAllocEx}, and \texttt{WriteProcessMemory}.
    - High entropy detected in \texttt{.text} section.
    - Strings include URLs and encoded PowerShell commands.

    \texttt{Retrieved Document [Malpedia: AgentTesla]}: \emph{'AgentTesla often
        uses process injection via RemoteThread API and obfuscates its communication
        channels through encoded PowerShell'}

    \texttt{Task:}
    Based on the static features and retrieved documentation, classify the malware into a known family.
    Justify your classification and explain how it aligns or diverges from similar threats.
\end{quote}

\subsection{Prompt Filtering and Safety Checks}\label{subsec:PromptFiltering}
Before submission to the LLM, the prompt is passed through a compliance module. This ensures:

\begin{itemize}
    \item No raw hex dumps or disassembly snippets are embedded directly
    \item No private information (e.g., internal IPs, analyst notes) is leaked
    \item Token length does not exceed the model's maximum context window
\end{itemize}

This is particularly important for mitigating hallucination and injection risks, as highlighted in
PoisonedRAG~\cite{zou2024poisonedrag}.

\subsection{Model Selection and Inference Mode}\label{subsec:InferenceMode}
Prompts are submitted in inference-only mode to two different models for comparative analysis:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{GPT-4o via OpenAI API}: Provides strong generalization,
          reasoning, and explanation capabilities. Used as the primary evaluation model.
    \item \textbf{DeepSeek-R1 via API}: Offers a strong open-weight baseline,
          used to evaluate model consistency and dependency on proprietary tuning.
\end{itemize}

Both models are used without fine-tuning, relying solely on the richness and clarity of the prompt
to drive classification behavior. Prompt-response pairs are logged for post-analysis, including
model temperature, latency, and token usage.

\subsection{Task Types}\label{subsec:TaskTypes}
Three main task templates are implemented:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Classification-Only}: For determining the most probable
          malware family based on feature and document alignment.
    \item \textbf{Classification + Justification}: Adds reasoning requirements,
          forcing the model to cite evidence from retrieved sources or static
          features.
    \item \textbf{Classification + Comparison}: Requests explanation of how the
          input sample compares with known variants, useful for script-based or
          ambiguous binaries.
\end{enumerate}

This multi-template design allows a fine-grained analysis of LLM behavior across different
cognitive demands.

\subsection{Reproducibility and Prompt Versioning}\label{subsec:PromptVersioning}
All prompts are versioned using a hash-based identifier computed from the static feature set and
retrieved document IDs. This ensures that any inference result can be reproduced precisely, even if
the underlying documents or model APIs are updated. Prompt versions are stored alongside inference
results and used during evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:EvaluationFramework}).

\section{Evaluation Framework}\label{sec:EvaluationFramework}
% Define your evaluation metrics: accuracy, efficiency, interpretability.
% Describe baseline comparisons against traditional static malware classifiers (if applicable).
% Discuss any human-in-the-loop components for evaluating explanation quality.
This section defines the evaluation framework used to compare the baseline and \gls{rag}-enhanced
pipelines. The evaluation focuses on three axes: classification accuracy, processing efficiency,
and interpretability of the results. Both quantitative and qualitative metrics are employed to
assess how well each pipeline performs in realistic analysis scenarios.

\subsection{Evaluation Criteria}\label{subsec:EvaluationCriteria}
The following core dimensions are used to evaluate pipeline outputs:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Accuracy}: Measures the correctness of malware family
          classification. Ground truth is derived from VirusTotal consensus labels,
          harmonized via a mapping layer to canonical family names. This is used as a
          benchmark label for each sample.

    \item \textbf{Efficiency}: Assessed by measuring average processing time per
          sample, from ingestion to classification. For the \gls{llm} pipeline, this
          includes embedding, retrieval, prompt construction, and inference latency.
          For the baseline, it includes decompilation, feature logging, heuristic
          evaluation, and API enrichment.

    \item \textbf{Interpretability}: Evaluated through the presence, structure,
          and relevance of classification justifications. The \gls{llm} pipeline is
          expected to produce natural language rationales, while the baseline outputs
          triggered rules and matched tags. Interpretability is quantified using
          metrics inspired by Wong et al.~\cite{wong2021practice} and Sahoo et
          al.~\cite{sahoo2024systematic}, including:
          \begin{itemize}
              \item Explanation coverage (fraction of samples with generated explanation)
              \item Contextual grounding (presence of retrieved document citations)
              \item Rule-reference matching (for baseline heuristics)
          \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Pipeline Output Comparison}\label{subsec:OutputComparison}
All pipeline outputs are stored in a unified format including:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Classification label
    \item Confidence score or LLM certainty expression
    \item Explanation content (if available)
    \item Processing time and component-level latency
\end{itemize}

This structure allows direct comparison between the baseline and the LLM-based results, aligned by
sample ID making it easy to spot divergencies.

\subsection{Baseline Alignment and Heuristic Audit}\label{subsec:HeuristicAudit}
To assess the internal validity of the baseline pipeline, its rule set is evaluated independently
on a manually curated subset of samples. Labels and triggered rules are reviewed for semantic
consistency and overlap. Samples with ambiguous or conflicting tags are excluded from accuracy
scoring but retained for interpretability analysis.

\subsection{Limitations of Evaluation}\label{subsec:EvalLimitations}
While the framework aims for reproducibility and objectivity, it is subject to limitations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ground truth labels rely on VirusTotal consensus, which may be incomplete or inconsistent across
          vendors.
    \item No human annotation or adversarial robustness testing is performed.
    \item Explanation interpretability metrics are based on surface-level patterns and not semantic accuracy.
\end{itemize}

These limitations are discussed further in Section~\ref{sec:ReliabilityValidityLimitations}.

\section{Reliability, Validity, and Limitations}\label{sec:ReliabilityValidityLimitations}

This section outlines the steps taken to ensure experimental reproducibility, addresses internal
and external validity, and describes the primary limitations of the study.

\subsection{Reproducibility}\label{subsec:Reproducibility}
Reproducibility is ensured through the following strategies:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Version-Controlled Codebase}: All pipeline code, configuration
          files, and documentation are maintained in a public Git
          repository.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/andremmfaria/rexis}}
    \item \textbf{Fixed Random Seeds}: Where applicable (e.g., document
          shuffling, API sampling), fixed seeds are applied to reduce stochastic
          variance.
    \item \textbf{Containerized Environment}: All software dependencie s are
          managed via \texttt{pdm} and deployed in Docker-based containers, allowing
          identical execution across systems.
    \item \textbf{Explicit API Versions}: External services such as OpenAI and
          DeepSeek are called using versioned API endpoints and documented model
          identifiers (e.g., \texttt{gpt-4o}, \texttt{deepseek-coder}).
    \item \textbf{Metadata Logging}: All pipeline executions produce structured
          logs with timestamps, sample IDs, tool versions, and model metadata.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Internal Validity}\label{subsec:InternalValidity}
Internal validity is maintained by isolating experimental variables and using consistent input
samples across pipelines. Static features are extracted using a shared Ghidra backend. Document
retrieval, prompt templates, and embedding models are fixed during experimentation. No manual
intervention occurs in classification workflows.

Potential threats include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Heuristic Bias}: Rule-based logic may overfit known patterns
          and underperform on obfuscated or novel samples.
    \item \textbf{Retrieval Drift}: Embedding-based retrieval may vary with
          changes in the document corpus or chunking strategy.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{External Validity}\label{subsec:ExternalValidity}
Findings are limited to \gls{pe}-based malware and static analysis contexts. Results may not
generalize to dynamic behavior-based classification, fileless malware, or non-Windows platforms.
The use of inference-only LLMs avoids fine-tuning, which may affect performance in real-world
analyst environments.

\subsection{Study Limitations}\label{subsec:StudyLimitations}
Several constraints impact the scope of the study. These limitations are considered when
interpreting results in the final evaluation.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Static Analysis Only}: No dynamic or hybrid analysis is
          performed. Obfuscated or packed binaries may yield incomplete features.
    \item \textbf{LLM Limitations}: Model responses may include hallucinated or
          overconfident statements~\cite{zou2024poisonedrag, ferrag2024allinsights}.
          Prompts are filtered but not adversarially tested.
    \item \textbf{Dataset Scale}: Sample sizes are constrained by API quotas and
          corpus curation effort. This limits statistical power and coverage of edge
          cases.
    \item \textbf{No Adversarial Robustness}: The system is not evaluated
          against prompt injection, retrieval poisoning, or obfuscation-specific
          evasion~\cite{zou2024poisonedrag}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Ethical Considerations}\label{sec:EthicalConsiderations}
% Ensure ethical use of malware datasets.
% Describe safety protocols: no execution of live malware, all samples are statically analyzed.
% Highlight responsible use of LLMs and privacy-conscious data handling if using
% VirusTotal.
The research presented in this study involves the use of malware samples, static code artifacts,
and third-party threat intelligence sources. Several precautions are taken to ensure that the study
adheres to ethical standards in cybersecurity research.

\subsection{Safe Handling of Malware Samples}
All malware samples are statically analyzed without execution. No sandboxing or dynamic behavior
monitoring is performed. Samples are stored in a secure, isolated environment with no network
access or file execution permissions. The analysis pipeline operates solely on extracted features
(e.g., strings, imports, entropy) and decompiled code sections.

Samples are sourced from public research repositories such as \gls{malwarebazaar} and
\gls{virustotal}, which provide malware binaries and metadata specifically for research and
detection engineering purposes. No private or unknown-origin malware is introduced into the
dataset.

\subsection{Use of Public and Licensed APIs}
All external APIs (OpenAI, DeepSeek, VirusTotal, MalwareBazaar) are accessed through official
channels with proper authentication and rate limits. API usage complies with the respective Terms
of Service, and no bulk data scraping or unauthorized reverse engineering is conducted.

VirusTotal’s API is used strictly for metadata enrichment and tagging. No personal data,
user-uploaded files, or proprietary content is accessed or stored.

\subsection{Privacy and Data Anonymity}
No personal, user-identifiable, or system-specific data is involved in the research. All data used
is either public (e.g., APT reports) or anonymized (e.g., sample hashes). Retrieved documents are
cleaned to remove IP addresses, hostnames, or other sensitive references prior to embedding.

\subsection{Responsible Use of LLMs}
The use of \glspl{llm} in this study is limited to inference-only interactions. No fine-tuning is
performed. The prompts are explicitly filtered to exclude shellcode, network addresses, or exploit
payloads. The system is designed for classification and explanation, not for generating or
manipulating malware.

To prevent propagation of misleading or unsafe content, all generated responses are stored and
reviewed offline. In alignment with recommendations from recent LLM safety
surveys~\cite{ferrag2024allinsights, metta2024generativeai}, the system avoids code synthesis and
adheres to a read-only analysis posture.

\section{Summary}\label{sec:MethodologySummary}

This chapter described the methodological framework adopted to evaluate the use of
retrieval-augmented \glspl{llm} for static malware analysis. The research design follows an
experimental, prototype-based approach that compares a traditional static classification pipeline
against a semantically enriched \gls{llm}-driven alternative.

Two parallel pipelines were detailed: a heuristic+VirusTotal-based baseline, and an enhanced
pipeline that uses semantic retrieval and structured prompt construction for \gls{llm} inference. Both
pipelines operate on the same \gls{pe} samples and use \gls{ghidra}-based feature extraction to
ensure consistency. An ingestion pipeline was presented to populate a vectorized corpus of threat
intelligence documents, enabling semantically grounded inference.

The environment is implemented in Python using modular, reproducible tooling. Prompt engineering
strategies, evaluation metrics, and ethical safeguards were also outlined. All experiments are
version-controlled and designed to support reproducibility and interpretability.

This methodology enables the research questions to be addressed from both a technical and
analytical perspective, supporting comparison across accuracy, explanation quality, and pipeline
adaptability. The next chapter presents the implementation details and experimental results derived
from executing this methodology.
